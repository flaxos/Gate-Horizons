# Chapter 1 — The Moon That Lied

They named it HERMES-K because naming things made them feel owned.

For a week it was just another line in an automated survey report: an irregular object in a quiet patch of the belt, a few kilometres across, slow rotation, dusty albedo, nothing that would make the news. The cataloguing software flagged it only because its light curve wouldn’t sit still. Not noise—pattern. Like the thing was trying to answer a question nobody had asked.

Mara Lin didn’t believe in haunted rocks. Mara believed in budgets, error bars, and the blunt tyranny of delta-v. The universe didn’t do drama unless you earned it.

Still, she pulled the raw data anyway.

The brightness oscillation had a beat: two dominant frequencies, slightly off-phase, that implied a shape the object did not have. The curve didn’t match a tumbling rubble pile. It didn’t match a binary companion either. The (boring) options were: the measurements were wrong, or the object was lying.

She opened a channel to the instrument team.

“Your calibration’s clean?” she asked.

The reply came back a minute later, clipped and annoyed. “Clean. Cleaner than my apartment. Why?”

“Because I’ve got a dustball that’s behaving like it has an internal reflector.”

Silence. Then: “That’s not a thing.”

Mara stared at the plot again. “Apparently it is.”

In the following days, the object became everyone’s problem. Not because it was mysterious—mysteries were cheap. It became a problem because the anomaly persisted through independent datasets. Different telescopes. Different wavelengths. Different teams who would have happily watched each other fail.

HERMES-K stayed wrong.

Public interest arrived like it always did: late, loud, and certain. A journalist called it a “moonlet”—as if giving it a softer name would make it more polite. Someone on a network show called it a “message.” Someone else called it an “omen.” The agency’s comms team begged for silence and got the opposite.

A month later, they approved the flyby.

The probe was a pragmatic thing—more bus than spaceship, assembled from standard modules and a hard promise to survive long enough to send its findings home. Its cameras were simple. Its instruments were not. They bolted on magnetometers sensitive enough to notice the universe thinking about iron.

When the probe finally fell toward the object’s orbital track, its thrusters fired in short bursts that sounded like nothing and cost everything. The belt wasn’t far by cosmic standards, but cosmic standards didn’t pay for fuel.

The first images came in grainy, high-contrast, and thoroughly unimpressive.

A lumpy body. Grey and brown. A dust halo that clung to it like static. Scar tissue from old impacts, craterlets stacked on craterlets like the world had been used for target practice.

“Rubble pile,” someone muttered in the control room. “Told you.”

Mara said nothing. Her eyes were on the magnetometer readout creeping upward.

There shouldn’t have been a field worth mentioning. Not at this size, not with this composition. Not without a coherent core.

The data ticked higher.

A technician leaned forward. “Could be solar wind interactions.”

Mara shook her head slowly. “Not like that.”

They watched the probe skim within a few kilometres. Dust glinted and spun away from the hull, lazily, as if time itself had thickened. On the screen, the object rotated, and the light curve did what it always did—refused to behave.

Then the probe’s radar sounder fired.

A pulse. A return. A second return.

The first return was expected: the surface, a chaotic mess of particulate and fractured rock, scattering the signal into static.

The second return was wrong.

It was too clean. Too sharp. Too smooth. A curved boundary where no curved boundary belonged.

Mara felt the control room change around her—the way a crowd changes when someone realises the thing on stage isn’t a trick.

The flight engineer whispered, almost reverently, “That’s… a cavity.”

“Rubble piles don’t do cavities like that,” Mara said, and heard her own voice come out tight.

They ran it again. Changed frequency. Changed angle. More returns. More wrongness.

It wasn’t a random void. It was consistent. Deliberate in its geometry. And it wasn’t centred like a natural hollow might be. It sat offset, like a seed.

Over the next hours, the probe’s instruments mapped the inside in slices and gradients: the body was a mess of dust and fractured stone with a single quiet truth buried in it—a smooth, ringlike structure that refused to scatter radar the way rock should.

A torus.

A ring, but not in the romantic sense. In the industrial sense. In the sense of something built to hold stresses.

Mara knew what a manufactured object looked like in data. Manufactured objects had edges that nature didn’t bother with. Manufactured objects had symmetry that did not apologise.

She looked at the torus’ dimensions and felt her scalp prickle.

“Who made it?” someone asked.

“Does hookup matter?” another voice shot back. “We don’t even know if it’s… what you think it is.”

Mara didn’t answer either of them. Her mind had gone to the same place it always did when confronted with an impossibility.

What do we have to do to test it without dying?

Because that was the rule. That was the only rule. You didn’t touch strange machinery with bravado. You touched it with scaffolding, redundancy, and a willingness to be disappointed slowly.

Back on Earth, they convened panels and committees and emergency closed sessions full of people who believed authority could bend physics. Networks ran loops of the lumpy dustball and spoke about destiny. Stock prices moved, because of course they did.

They sent a second mission with more patience than the first: a lander that could drill, anchor, and listen.

The landing was a ballet done with thruster fumes and terror. Low gravity meant everything bounced. Dust meant nothing stayed clean. Electrostatics meant the lander wore its environment like a coating.

The drill went down metre by metre. Samples came up exactly as boring as expected: fine regolith, fractured stone, ancient carbonaceous mix.

Then the seismics began.

They placed sensors across the surface like a spider laying out a web. They fired small charges. They listened.

The waves that moved through the body should have scattered, diffused, died in the chaos.

Instead, the moonlet rang.

The waveform didn’t just propagate. It reflected. It found that smooth boundary and came back clean.

Mara watched the plots scroll and felt the certainty settle in like a weight.

This thing had been here a long time. Long enough for dust to collect around it. Long enough for impacts to bury it. Long enough for humans to mistake it for a natural object until their instruments got fussy.

Infrastructure.

A door, maybe. Or a lock.

In the control room, somebody finally said the quiet part out loud.

“If it’s a gate,” they said, voice thin, “where does it go?”

Nobody answered.

Sol had always felt enormous. Yet if the torus inside HERMES-K was engineered, the system wasn’t a cradle.

It was a cul-de-sac.

The director’s message arrived an hour later, stamped with layers of encryption and the bureaucratic tone of someone trying not to sound frightened.

All data is now classified.  
A multinational consortium will be formed.  
Further missions are authorised.  
Do not speculate publicly.

Mara stared at the last line and almost laughed.

Speculation was what humans did when they smelled a horizon.

Outside the window, the belt drifted in indifferent silence.

Inside the moon, something waited—dead, buried, and stubbornly real.

And if humanity was about to wake it, they’d better learn the difference between opening a door and inviting whatever was on the other side to notice you exist.
